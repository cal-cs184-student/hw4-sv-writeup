Overview

In this homework, I implemented a cloth simulator that rendered the wireframe of a cloth composed of point masses and springs, the physics of cloth around objects (spheres and planes), as well as implementing shading techniques, both new and from previous homeworks. I found the most interesting aspects of the assignment the different parameters that could be changed during simulation such as the spring constant, damping, and density; it was cool to see how the simulation behaved under different conditions and I learned more about physics as well.

Part 1: Masses and springs

Above is the wireframe comprised of point masses and springs. By only turning on the shearing constraint, point masses are only diagonally connected which is displayed in the wireframe. By only turning off the shearing constraint, we see that point masses are horizontally and vertically connected. By turning on all constraints the connections are compounded and the point masses are connected to each other in all directions (horizontal, vertical, diagonal).

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

This is the cloth in its resting state with default parameters.

When changing the density, we effectively make the cloth heavier and thus it displays as further weighed down by gravity. At a low density value, the cloth appears smoother and has less wrinkles/folds. At a high density value, the cloth has several more wrinkles/folds indicating that it is getting weighed down more.

Similarly, when changing the spring constant we change the 'stiffness' of the cloth. With a higher spring constant the higher the cloth's stiffness, and the cloth appears very stiff with minimal folds. At a low spring constant, the cloth is very flexible and holds several more folds.

When changing the damping value, we change how much momentum the cloth holds when it falls. With greater damping, the cloth falls with less speed and also has a smaller range of motion. In contrast, a smaller damping value causes the cloth to fall with greater speed and range of motion, swinging back and forth for a longer period of time.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

To implement collisions with spheres, I first checked whether the point mass' position was inside of the sphere by checking whether the squared length of the radial (pm.position - origin) is less than the squared radius (radius2). If so, then we calculate the tangent point by projecting the radial onto the sphere surface. Finally, I update the position of the point mass by adding its correction (the difference between the last position and the tangent point), and adjusting it for friction by scaling with (1-friction).

As we saw in the cloth simulation without colliding with objects, changing the spring constant will affect the cloth's flexibility. With a lower spring constant, the cloth is very flexible and drapes more closely to the sphere. With a higher spring constant, the cloth does not bend as much and takes a flatter shape, only slightly draping itself over the sphere.

Similar to the sphere, to implement collisions with planes we first check whether the point mass position crossed the plane by computing the dot products (pm.position - point) • normal and (pm.last_position - point) • normal, checking if the two results are opposite signs. Then, like the sphere we calculate its projected point with pm.position - ((pm.position - point) • normal) * normal, apply the offset and scale for friction, updating the point mass position with the result.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

I first start the implementation of self collisions by implementing hash_position, which takes a 3D Vector position and returns what mapped 3D box it is. This is done by dividing each coordinate by the box dimensions, which were calculated with 3 * width (or height) / num_width_points (or num_height_points), with the z dimension being the max between width and height, and taking the floor of the result. I then used hash_position within build_spatial_map to go iterate each point mass and map it to its corresponding box. With the spatial map constructed, self_collide first gets the box at the point mass' hash position and uses the other point masses in the box as potential collision candidates. If the candidate is within the minimum distance 2 * thickness, we calculate the correction by first getting the difference between the point mass and the candidate's position (separation), dividing it by its norm (direction) and multiplying the direction with the minumum distance 2*thickness - the separation norm. We add this result to the total corrections, as well as add it to the amount of total collisions. If the candidate is itself, or if the cadidate is pinned, we skip and do not see them as correction targets. Finally, we calculate the average corrections using the aforementioned total correction and total collision values along with simulation_steps, and use the result to update the point mass position.

As described in previous parts, the density affects the weight of the cloth and the spring constant affects the flexibility of the cloth. A high density/low ks causes it to be heavier or more flexible than the plane, making it fall through. In contrast, a low density/high ks allows it to even out and hold less wrinkles over time.
